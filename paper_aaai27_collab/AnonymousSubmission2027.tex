\begin{abstract}
% AI agents for rare-disease diagnosis increasingly combine large language models (LLMs) with multi-agent orchestration, retrieval, structured phenotype reasoning, and simulated clinical interaction. Yet published systems are evaluated on incompatible datasets and protocols, making cross-system claims difficult to verify and obscuring whether gains arise from agent scaffolding, model choice, or case selection. We introduce RareAgentBench, an agent-native benchmark that standardizes four capabilities---phenotype extraction, phenotype-only and genotype-aware differential diagnosis, and clinical-communication faithfulness---through a canonical case schema and system-specific adapters for structured HPO and free-text inputs. The benchmark draws on 84,450 cases from five sources, including a time-based PMC holdout, and preregisters 11 hypotheses and 12 ablations. We compare eight diagnostic systems, including classical and offline methods, with three unscaffolded LLM controls. Our evaluation on RareAgentBench yields four key findings: (1) on HPO-input datasets, classical and offline methods outperform all scaffolded LLM agents (best R@1: 0.47 vs. 0.30, a 17-point gap); (2) multi-agent scaffolding does not reliably beat a single-LLM control: light scaffolds change R@1 by at most 1.4 points, whereas heavier scaffolds reduce it by 8 to 36 points; (3) higher cost does not guarantee higher accuracy---DeepSeek V4-Flash is about $11\times$ cheaper than Gemini Flash but 3--8 points less accurate on HPO inputs, whereas GPT-5 with minimal reasoning costs about $26\times$ more than V4-Flash without a consistent advantage; and (4) structured variant information improves R@1 by about 20 points across systems. We release the benchmark, adapters, execution receipts, and leaderboard to support reproducible and cost-aware evaluation of rare-disease diagnostic agents.

Rare-disease diagnosis is a high-impact setting in which AI systems must reason over sparse phenotypes, genomic evidence, and noisy clinical narratives. Yet existing diagnostic agents are evaluated on incompatible datasets and protocols, making it difficult to separate the effects of agent scaffolding, model choice, input evidence, and case selection. We introduce RareAgentBench, an agent-native, contamination-aware evaluation framework that normalizes a source pool of 84,450 candidate instances from five sources into a canonical case schema. It evaluates phenotype extraction, phenotype-only and genotype-aware differential diagnosis, and communication faithfulness under shared evidence budgets, metrics, and failure accounting, including a 198-case post-cutoff PMC holdout. Using preregistered hypotheses and ablations, we compare five scaffolded agent architectures across four LLM backbones with matched single-LLM and classical or offline baselines. On structured HPO inputs, LIRICAL and VC-RDAgent achieve Recall@1 of 0.47 and 0.44, respectively, versus 0.30 for the best scaffolded LLM agent. Relative to matched single-LLM controls, light scaffolds change Recall@1 by at most 1.4 percentage points, whereas heavier scaffolds reduce it by 8–36 points across two datasets. Structured variant information provides the most consistent gain, a genotype-channel lift of approximately 20 points. Meanwhile, API cost and latency vary by up to 26× and 80× without a consistent accuracy advantage. These results show that evidence channels and evaluation design matter more than orchestration complexity in the evaluated settings. We release the benchmark, adapters, execution receipts, and leaderboard to support reproducible, cost-aware evaluation of rare-disease diagnostic agents.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Rare diseases are individually uncommon but collectively affect an estimated
3.5--5.9\% of the global population
\citep{NguengangWakap2020Prevalence}. For patients and families, reaching a
diagnosis can require years of referrals, repeated testing, and
misdiagnosis. In a 41-country survey of 6,507 people living with rare
diseases, \citet{Faye2024DiagnosisDelay} reported a mean diagnostic delay of
4.7 years. An unsupported differential can redirect testing, postpone
specialist referral, and prolong this diagnostic odyssey. Computational
decision support must therefore be evaluated for reliability, evidence use,
and resource cost as well as accuracy.

Rare-disease diagnosis is a natural target for medical agents because it is a
multi-step evidence-integration problem. A system may need to extract and
normalize phenotypes, retrieve disease knowledge, reconcile phenotype and
genotype evidence, and revise a differential as new information arrives.
Recent systems accordingly augment large language models (LLMs) with
deliberation, adaptive collaboration, sequential interaction, retrieval, and
phenotype--genotype reasoning
\citep{tang2024medagents,kim2024mdagents,nori2025sequential,
schmidgall2026agentclinic,zhao2026deeprare,liu2026vcrdagent}. These designs
are promising, but additional scaffolds and tools also introduce failure
modes and inference cost. Their benefit can be established only by comparing
the complete pipeline with an appropriate matched control.

Existing studies vary in case mix, evidence representation, disease ontology,
backbone, tools, failure policy, and matching rule. A higher Recall@1 may
therefore reflect an easier dataset or more informative input rather than a
stronger agent. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows this fragmentation before
scaffolding is even introduced: the same no-scaffold method scores near zero
on RareBench but reaches 0.48--0.61 on the PMC-OA holdout across four
backbones. This dataset-dependent shift makes isolated headline scores
unsuitable for cross-paper comparison and motivates a unified benchmark with
matched cases, evidence, backbones, and scoring. Contamination and
reproducibility risks further strengthen this need
\citep{nguyen2026diagnostic}.

A useful benchmark must therefore satisfy three requirements: compare each
agent with a no-scaffold control under the same backbone; standardize cases
and permitted evidence to separate information gains from orchestration
gains; and retain failures while measuring latency and cost alongside
accuracy. Because retrieval, orchestration, parsing, and failure handling all
affect the prediction, the complete pipeline---not the backbone alone---must
be the unit of evaluation. Classical phenotype-based methods must also remain
in scope because they are clinically relevant comparators and can outperform
general-purpose LLMs on structured inputs
\citep{robinson2020lirical,reese2026systematic}.

We introduce \textbf{RareAgentBench}, an agent-native framework for controlled
evaluation of rare-disease diagnostic systems. Five source collections form
four evidence layers spanning curated phenotypes, de-identified electronic
health record notes, case-report text, and a 198-case post-cutoff holdout
\citep{danis2025phenopackets,chen2024rarebench,chen2026rarearena}. A shared
\texttt{CanonicalCase} schema preserves provenance, parallel disease
identifiers, and task-specific availability masks. System-specific adapters
expose only permitted evidence and standardize predictions; per-case receipts
record status, failures, token use, price-table-derived cost, and latency.
Empty outputs, parser errors, and timeouts remain in the denominator. Our
primary evaluation focuses on phenotype-based differential diagnosis, with
phenotype extraction, variant-aware diagnosis, and communication
faithfulness treated as exploratory extensions.

In the primary matched diagnostic evaluation, established systems remain
strong: on structured HPO inputs, LIRICAL and VC-RDAgent achieve Recall@1 of
0.47 and 0.44, respectively, compared with 0.30 for the best scaffolded LLM
agent. Relative to backbone-matched controls, lightweight scaffolds change
Recall@1 by at most 1.4 percentage points, whereas heavier scaffolds reduce
it by 8--36 points across two datasets. API cost and median latency vary by
as much as 26$\times$ and 80$\times$ without a consistent accuracy
advantage. Agent complexity alone is therefore not a reliable proxy for
diagnostic quality.

Our contributions are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An agent-native benchmark design} that represents complete
  pipelines through layered evidence, controlled views, and a post-cutoff
  holdout.
  \item \textbf{A matched and auditable protocol} with backbone-matched
  controls, explicit failure denominators, and per-case receipts.
  \item \textbf{Cross-system evidence under a common diagnostic setting}
  separating scaffold effects from dataset, backbone, and evaluator choices.
  \item \textbf{Reproducible infrastructure for follow-up evaluation},
  including the harness, adapters, predictions, and task interfaces.
\end{itemize}

RareAgentBench is an evaluation resource, not an autonomous diagnostic
system. Its intended social benefit is to make rare-disease AI claims more
comparable and auditable and to direct prospective validation toward systems
that use patient evidence reliably.
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Rare-Disease Diagnostic Models and Benchmarks}

Rare-disease decision support predates LLMs and commonly ranks candidate
diseases by combining phenotypic similarity, inheritance, and genomic
evidence. For example, \citet{robinson2020lirical} introduced an interpretable
likelihood-ratio framework, while \citet{mao2025phenobrain} developed a
phenotype-based pipeline for differential diagnosis from electronic health
records. These systems remain important comparators: in a systematic
benchmark, \citet{reese2026systematic} found that established rare-disease
decision-support tools still outperformed general-purpose LLMs on structured
case representations.

Recent datasets have expanded evaluation of LLM-based diagnosis.
RareBench and RareArena assess rare-disease knowledge and differential
diagnosis across curated clinical cases
\citep{chen2024rarebench,chen2026rarearena}. The GA4GH Phenopacket corpus
provides standardized, case-level phenotype and genomic representations
\citep{danis2025phenopackets}, while other work studies multilingual
robustness, reasoning, realistic electronic health records, and multimodal
evidence
\citep{chimirri2025consistent,qiu2025medrbench,eizaldin2026mimicrd,
ning2026mmrarebench}. Collectively, these resources have advanced
model-centric evaluation, but they use different case distributions, evidence
formats, disease vocabularies, and matching rules. They therefore do not by
themselves support controlled comparisons of complete agent pipelines.

\subsection{Benchmarks for LLM and Medical Agents}

Agent benchmarks evaluate capabilities that are not captured by static
question answering. SWE-bench measures whether agents can resolve real-world
software issues, AgentBoard analyzes multi-turn behavior, and
$\tau$-bench tests interactions among an agent, tools, and a simulated user
\citep{jimenez2024swebench,ma2024agentboard,yao2025taubench}. In medicine,
MedAgentBench provides a virtual electronic health record environment, and
AgentClinic evaluates tool-using agents in simulated multimodal clinical
encounters \citep{jiang2025medagentbench,schmidgall2026agentclinic}.

Evaluation frameworks also argue against reducing system quality to a single
accuracy number. HELM evaluates models across multiple scenarios and
desiderata \citep{liang2023helm}, and MedHELM extends holistic
evaluation to medical tasks \citep{bedi2026medhelm}. This principle is
particularly important for diagnostic agents, whose practical value depends
on failure rate, latency, resource use, and evidence fidelity in addition to
ranking accuracy. Moreover, automated evaluation can itself introduce bias:
\citet{panickssery2024selfpreference} showed that LLM evaluators may favor
their own generations, whereas \citet{verga2024juries} studied panels of
diverse models as an alternative to a single judge. These benchmarks and
evaluation methods motivate our multi-metric protocol, but none is designed
to control the distinctive phenotype, genotype, and ontology choices in
rare-disease diagnosis.

\subsection{Rare-Disease Diagnostic Agents}

Medical agent systems use collaboration or sequential interaction to extend
the capabilities of a backbone model. MedAgents assigns clinical roles to
multiple LLMs, MDAgents adapts the collaboration structure to problem
complexity, and sequential-diagnosis systems request additional information
over multiple turns
\citep{tang2024medagents,kim2024mdagents,nori2025sequential}. Rare-disease
systems further incorporate specialist teams, retrieval, structured clinical
records, phenotype--genotype reasoning, or virtual case construction
\citep{chen2026rareagents,zhao2026deeprare,wu2025rdma,
liu2026vcrdagent}.

These studies demonstrate promising agent designs, but their reported results
combine the effects of scaffolding with changes in backbone model, cases,
available evidence, tools, and scoring. Consequently, it remains unclear when
an agent architecture improves on the same LLM under matched conditions, when
gains instead come from structured phenotype or variant inputs, and whether
additional inference cost is justified. RareAgentBench addresses this gap by
treating the full pipeline as the unit of evaluation while holding the case
view and evaluator fixed, logging failures and resource use, and comparing
each scaffold with a matched single-LLM control.

\section{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec-4}

RareAgentBench treats the complete diagnostic pipeline, rather than the
backbone alone, as the unit of evaluation. Its central design is a matched
comparison: wherever protocols are compatible, an agent scaffold is compared
with an unscaffolded control under the same backbone, case set, permitted
evidence, and evaluator. Per-case logging separates scaffold effects from
evidence modality and resource use, while retaining failed attempts in every
reported denominator.

  \centering

    Figures/figure1-1.png}
  \caption{RareAgentBench overview. (a) Source-specific filtering constructs
  four evidence layers; (b) \texttt{CanonicalCase} masks define eligible task
  views; and (c) matched controls and agents produce auditable per-case
  receipts.}
  \label{fig:rareagentbench-overview}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Data construction and leakage control
(Figure~\ref{fig:rareagentbench-overview}(a)).}
Each layer is defined by its selection procedure, not only by its source.
\textbf{L1} ingests 10,051 Phenopacket Store files but excludes packets
without a usable source-native OMIM or ORPHA diagnosis; RareBench-HF adds
1,122 cases across four splits
\citep{danis2025phenopackets,chen2024rarebench}. No cross-source
deduplication is currently applied.

\textbf{L2} is a de-leaked MIMIC-IV-Note funnel rather than a direct cohort
slice \citep{johnson2024mimiciv31}. Starting from 150,033 admissions carrying
a rare-disease code, we retain exact ICD-10--Orphanet mappings (107,144),
active rare disorders (18,392), rare principal diagnoses (2,325), and
admissions with a discharge summary (1,255). We then exclude 172 notes whose
masked mention occurs in a prior-history context and apply an
Orphanet-synonym screen, leaving 687 admissions. The reported 416-case probe
is the subset whose gold disease has an Orphanet phenotype annotation
\citep{orphadata2026nomenclature}; these HPO terms are disease-level rather
than case-level evidence. Notes are truncated before the first
diagnosis-revealing header, and gold names and recognized synonyms are
masked. A full-corpus assertion verifies that the verbatim gold name is absent
from the model input, although paraphrased prior history cannot be ruled out.

\textbf{L3} retains 8,562 RareArena RDS records in the current release,
representing task instances rather than unique patients; the RDC confirmation
view is not present in the frozen data \citep{chen2026rarearena}.
\textbf{L4} is a 198-case temporal holdout: 2,401 PubMed candidates yield
1,433 mapped records, from which the top-ranked 250 are screened and 52
non-post-cutoff reports are removed. Its current labels are provisional
silver labels produced through model-assisted extraction and adjudication;
the prepared physician-review workbook has not yet been returned. The
untruncated MIMIC notes, evaluation-only gold fields, and local VCF files
remain on the authorized machine; cloud services receive only the
task-approved de-leaked representation.

\paragraph{Canonical cases, eligibility, and task views
(Figure~\ref{fig:rareagentbench-overview}(b)).}
Every retained record is normalized into a Pydantic-v2
\texttt{CanonicalCase} containing source, clinical text, observed and
excluded HPO terms, structured variants, parallel disease and gene
identifiers, demographics, provenance, and availability masks. These masks
make eligibility an evidence property: adapters cannot expose fields not
assigned to the current capability. Parallel OMIM, ORPHA, and source
identifiers support ontology-aware scoring, while original and generated
narratives remain distinguishable.

The interface defines four capabilities. \textbf{P1} extracts ranked HPO
terms from text; \textbf{P2} ranks diseases from the available phenotype
view; \textbf{P3} augments that view with structured variants; and
\textbf{P4} assesses whether the final answer is factual, relevant,
evidence-supported, and consistent with the trace. The frozen full matrix
currently contains P2 only. P1 and P3 are supported by separate 50-case L1
pilots, while P4 is an automated-judge pilot on L1 with ten cases per agent;
these are labeled exploratory rather than presented as full-matrix results.
The schema supports paired gold-HPO and end-to-end text views, but the frozen
receipts do not contain a complete same-case dual-pass matrix. We therefore
report the evidence modality supplied by each layer and do not claim a
benchmark-wide phenotype-extraction penalty.

\paragraph{Matched execution, receipts, and scoring
(Figure~\ref{fig:rareagentbench-overview}(c)).}
The evaluation includes two offline baselines, an unscaffolded control for
each of four backbones, and six agent systems. Matched cells share the
backbone, case set, evidence mask, and evaluator. Adapters are restricted to
transport, evidence projection, output parsing, and instrumentation; native
prompts, retrieval, tools, orchestration, and stopping rules are preserved
except for documented compatibility changes.

Each \texttt{PredictionLog} records run and case identity, system versions,
ranked outputs, status, tokens, price-table-derived cost, latency, and
available traces. An attempt succeeds only when its status is \texttt{ok} and
its ranked output is nonempty. Empty outputs, agent errors, parser errors, and
timeouts therefore remain in the denominator; protocol-level unavailability
is reported separately. Because the frozen matrix has one attempt per case,
$\mathrm{pass}^k$ is not reported, and calibration is omitted when confidence
values are synthesized rather than supplied by the system.

Disease scoring proceeds through exact identifier matching, OMIM--ORPHA
cross-mapping, direct disease-name matching, and finally fuzzy
name-to-Orphanet mapping at a threshold of 90. We do not claim a completed
manual threshold audit. In addition to disease Recall@$k$ and MRR, we report
failure rate, latency, and cost; supported subgroup analyses are limited to
prevalence, HPO density, sex, and partially covered organ-system strata.
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec-5}

\paragraph{Systems and backbones.}
\label{sec-5-2}
We compare six diagnostic systems---specifically five scaffolded agents and a
matched single-LLM control---together with two classical or offline baselines. Each LLM-based system is evaluated, where its
interaction protocol permits, with DeepSeek V4-Flash, DeepSeek V4-Pro,
Gemini 3 Flash, and GPT-5. Requests use version-pinned OpenRouter endpoints to
provide a common API, billing surface, and generation record
\citep{openrouter2026api}. Primary runs use the lowest reasoning setting
reliably supported by each endpoint: GPT-5 uses
\path{reasoning_effort=minimal}, and V4-Pro uses
\path{reasoning.enabled=false}; a reasoning-enabled single-LLM control is
reported separately. Temperature is 0 and the seed is 42 where exposed. Model
  identifiers, access dates, generation parameters, prices,
  software versions, and hardware for offline methods are recorded
  with each run.

\paragraph{Adapters and execution.}
\label{sec-5-3}
Each system runs in an isolated environment behind a common
\texttt{AgentAdapter}. The adapter receives a \texttt{CanonicalCase}, exposes
only the evidence permitted by the current capability and evaluation pass,
projects it into the system's native input, and returns a standardized
\texttt{PredictionLog}. Modifications are restricted to transport, input
projection, output parsing, and instrumentation; native prompts, retrieval,
tool policies, orchestration, and stopping rules are preserved. Per-case
receipts record the case, adapter and model versions, configuration hash,
ranked prediction, status, token use, cost, latency, and available traces.
Empty outputs, parser errors, and timeouts remain failures in the denominator;
protocol-incompatible cells are marked unavailable rather than dropped.
Gateway-reported costs are used when available, and
  token-based estimates are labeled explicitly. All
  system-specific deviations are recorded per case.

\paragraph{Metrics and statistical analysis.}
Primary metrics follow the definitions in Benchmark Design: F1 for phenotype
extraction,
disease Recall@1 for phenotype- and genotype-aware diagnosis, gene Top-$k$
accuracy for genotype-aware diagnosis, and the four-axis faithfulness score
for P4. Secondary outcomes include Recall@3/5/10, MRR, failure rate,
$\mathrm{pass}^k$, calibration, latency, and cost. Matched systems are compared
on the same cases using case-level paired analyses. We report bootstrap
confidence intervals and apply Holm--Bonferroni correction to the
prespecified confirmatory hypothesis family. Each result cell reports its
actual case denominator; $\mathrm{pass}^k$ is reported only where repeated
attempts under the same frozen configuration are available.

\paragraph{Human annotation and holdout governance.}
\label{sec-5-4}
Human review serves two purposes. First, the 198 temporal-holdout cases are
adjudicated for definitive diagnosis, phenotype fidelity, post-cutoff
publication date, and rare-disease status. Second, physicians rate P4 outputs
for factuality, relevance, evidential support, and consistency with the
recorded trace. We report inter-rater agreement using Cohen's $\kappa$ and
compare automated scores from Gemini- and Claude-family judges with physician
ratings to quantify judge-family sensitivity. The confirmatory metrics, multiplicity correction,
agent set, and one-time holdout evaluation were frozen before holdout
unblinding; exploratory analyses are labeled and excluded from confirmatory
hypothesis testing.

\section{Limitations and Social Impact}
\label{sec-9}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
RareAgentBench is a retrospective evaluation and does not measure prospective
diagnostic decisions, patient outcomes, or integration into clinical
workflows. Its cases are predominantly English-language and combine curated
repositories, published case reports, and an intensive-care EHR resource;
these sources may overrepresent distinctive presentations and provide
incomplete demographic metadata. Consequently, subgroup results do not
establish clinical fairness. The post-cutoff holdout reduces benchmark
exposure but cannot exclude contamination because backbone training corpora
are undisclosed. Phenotype extraction and P4 faithfulness also depend partly
on model-assisted labels and judges; physician review covers the designated
200 cases, and the observed judge-family sensitivity makes P4 exploratory.
Finally, minimal or disabled reasoning improves cross-system comparability but
may understate the capabilities of some frontier backbones. Adapter
incompatibilities, estimated costs for calls outside the common gateway, and
ontology-dependent matching further limit direct generalization beyond the
frozen configurations. Prospective, multilingual, and multimodal validation
remains necessary.

\paragraph{Social impact.}
Rare diseases impose long diagnostic delays, yet unreliable AI suggestions
can redirect testing, amplify disparities, or create false reassurance. The
intended benefit of RareAgentBench is therefore not automated diagnosis, but
more accountable evidence about when diagnostic AI is accurate, robust, and
worth its resource cost. Matched controls, explicit failure denominators,
subgroup reporting, and per-case receipts are intended to discourage
selective reporting and unsupported claims of agent benefit. Credentialed EHR
text and local genomic files remain local; external services receive only the
approved de-identified representation. Because public benchmarks can
encourage leaderboard optimization and underrepresent populations with less
digitized evidence, benchmark performance must not determine patient care or
access to specialist services. Any deployment requires independent
prospective evaluation, local governance, privacy review, clinician
oversight, and monitoring for unequal failure rates.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec-10}

RareAgentBench provides a controlled, agent-native evaluation of complete
rare-disease diagnostic pipelines across structured phenotypes, clinical
narratives, genetic variants, and a temporal holdout. Under matched inputs and
scoring, established offline systems remain strong: on structured HPO inputs,
LIRICAL and VC-RDAgent reach Recall@1 of 0.47 and 0.44, compared with 0.30 for
the best scaffolded LLM agent. Light scaffolds change Recall@1 by at most 1.4
percentage points, heavier scaffolds reduce it by 8--36 points, and structured
variants instead add approximately 20 points. Cost and latency vary by up to
26$\times$ and 80$\times$ without a consistent accuracy gain, while ontology
matching and judge choice materially change apparent performance.

These results argue for evaluating scaffolds against matched controls and for
treating failures, evidence fidelity, and resource use as first-order
outcomes. RareAgentBench is not a clinical deployment study; it is an
auditable foundation for identifying which systems merit prospective
validation and for making future claims about rare-disease AI more comparable
and reproducible.
